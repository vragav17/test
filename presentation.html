The problem

Two files. Same title. Nobody can say what's different.

A title does not ship once. It ships as a theatrical cut, an airline cut, a broadcast cut, a streaming master, a re-grade, a re-dub, a director's edition — and then again when a note comes back from compliance.

When two of those files land on a desk, the questions are always the same:

  • Is this actually a new version, or a re-wrap of one we already have?
  • What was cut, and at what timecode?
  • Did anyone touch the audio without touching the picture?
  • Is the difference editorial, or just the delivery format?

The metadata that should answer this — filenames, sidecar XML, the supplier's delivery note — is written by hand, and is wrong often enough that nobody trusts it.

WHAT ARRIVES
A
TITLE_MASTER_v3_FINAL.mxf
2 h 08 m · 1080p · SDR · 6 audio tracks
B
TITLE_TV_DELIVERY.mxf
2 h 04 m · 1080p · HDR10 · 6 audio tracks
Four minutes shorter. Different dynamic range. Is that one scene removed, or twelve trims spread across the film? Is the HDR flag a real re-grade or just a container difference?

Today this is answered by a human scrubbing both files side by side. It takes hours, it is done under deadline, and it is the first thing dropped when the deadline wins.

Why the obvious answers don't work

Every cheap shortcut fails on real deliveries.

01 — FILE HASH

Byte-compare the files

Answers one question: are these literally the same bytes. Re-encode the same edit at a different bitrate and the answer is "completely different", which is true and useless.

02 — RUNTIME

Compare durations

Tells you four minutes went missing. Not where, not how many places, and nothing at all when a scene is replaced by one of equal length.

03 — FRAME BY FRAME

Compare frame n of A to frame n of B

Works until the first cut. Remove ten seconds at minute four and every frame after it is misaligned — one edit reports as a two-hour difference.

04 — SLIDING WINDOW

Re-sync with a heuristic

Handles one shift. Breaks on the second, and on any reorder. Fails silently, in a way that looks like a clean result — the worst failure mode there is.

The real shape of the problem: two sequences of the same underlying content, with insertions, deletions, substitutions and reorders between them. That is not a video problem. It is a sequence alignment problem — and sequence alignment has an exact, optimal, sixty-year-old answer.

The approach

Turn each film into a string. Then align the strings.

Genome alignment compares two DNA sequences that share an ancestor and have since diverged by insertion, deletion and mutation. Two cuts of a film are the same structure with a different alphabet.

A shot is the right unit. It is editorially meaningful — cuts are where editors work — and there are a few thousand of them in a feature rather than a few hundred thousand frames.

The proxy earns its keep

Everything downstream reads one normalised 480p H.264 file instead of a 200 GB master: same resolution, same colour space, all audio tracks merged. Decode cost drops by orders of magnitude and the two versions become comparable by construction.

Lanes are independent

A and B share nothing until alignment, so they run on two workers at once — and a fingerprint computed once is cached and reused the next time that file is compared against anything else.

The technical probe skips the proxy

It reads the source, deliberately. The proxy is 8-bit BT.709 by definition, so probing it would report every HDR master on earth as SDR.

Stage 3–4 · the alphabet

What a 64-bit perceptual hash does and doesn't see.

imagehash.phash takes the shot's middle frame, reduces it to greyscale 32×32, runs a DCT, keeps the low-frequency 8×8 corner and thresholds it at the median. The result is 64 bits describing coarse luminance structure.

That makes it robust to exactly the things that should not count as an edit — resolution, bitrate, re-encoding — which is the whole point. But robustness cuts both ways, and the blind spots must be measured, not assumed.

Every number in this table was measured in this codebase, not quoted from a paper. Distance is out of 64 bits; ≤10 is a match, 11–20 is a weak band, >20 is a mismatch.

Stage 4 · the second signal

A re-dub changes nothing on screen.

A localised dub, a replaced music cue, a censored line — the picture is bit-identical and the picture hash says 0 / 64. Without a second signal these changes are invisible, and they are among the most common version differences that exist.

So every shot also carries a 64-bit audio hash: the audio under the shot at 22 050 Hz mono, a mel-spectrogram of 64 bands pooled to 8×8, thresholded at its median. Same width, same Hamming comparison, same units.

Audio never touches the alignment score. It is a separate pass over the pairs the alignment already matched: where the picture matches but the audio distance exceeds 16/64, the pair is retagged audio_changed.

That separation is deliberate. Letting an audio term into the scoring function would make the alignment's behaviour a matter of tuning two weights against each other — and the alignment is the part that has to be defensible.

WHY IT IS A SECOND PASS, NOT A SECOND TERM 
# align.py — the scoring function sees picture only
def score_matrix(a_bits, b_bits):
    d = hamming_matrix(a_bits, b_bits)
    s = np.full(d.shape, MISMATCH_SCORE)
    s[d <= WEAK_MAX_DISTANCE]  = WEAK_SCORE   # 0
    s[d <= MATCH_MAX_DISTANCE] = MATCH_SCORE  # +2
    return s

# ...and afterwards, over aligned pairs only:
if op.type == "equal" and ahash_distance > 16:
    op.type = "audio_changed"

The picture decides the timeline. Audio annotates it. Each signal is interpretable on its own, and a bad audio track can never corrupt the structural result.

Stage 5 · the judged component

Needleman-Wunsch — a real algorithm, not a heuristic.

Written directly against numpy. No bioinformatics library, no model, no tuned rules. It returns the provably optimal alignment under its scoring scheme — not a good-enough one.

H[i][j] = max(
    H[i-1][j-1] + score(aᵢ, bⱼ),   # pair them
    H[i-1][j]   + gap,            # a[i] deleted
    H[i][j-1]   + gap,            # b[j] inserted
)
ConditionScore
phash distance ≤ 10 — a match+2
11–20 — weak, neither rewarded nor punished0
> 20 — a mismatch−1
gap — an insertion or a deletion−1

The weak band matters: it lets two shots that are probably the same sit together without the alignment paying to keep them, which is how re-grades and overlays stop shredding the timeline.

THE ONE INTERESTING OPTIMISATION

The left-neighbour term H[i][j] = max(…, H[i][j−1] + gap) looks inherently sequential — each cell depends on the one beside it. With a linear gap penalty it is just a running maximum in disguise. Substituting

G[j] = H[i][j] − j·gap

⇒  G[j] = max(best[j] − j·gap, G[j−1])
⇒  np.maximum.accumulate(...)

Each row becomes three vector operations instead of an inner Python loop. A feature-length pair aligns in seconds, on a laptop, in pure numpy.

Zero references to any model in align.py. Verifiable with a single grep. The stage being judged is arithmetic, and it produces the same answer every time it runs.

Traceback walks the pointer matrix from the bottom-right corner, emitting one op per step. Consecutive ops of the same type are then grouped with itertools.groupby into regions carrying real timecodes on both sides, and equal runs are dropped.

Stage 7 · the output

Regions, with a timecode on both sides.

Every region answers three questions at once: what kind of change, where it sits in A, and where it sits in B. Both timecodes matter — an editor working in B cannot use A's timeline, and an archivist checking A cannot use B's.

TypeMeansReads as

Two artefacts, on purpose

report.json is the machine contract — stable field names, no embedded images, safe to diff or feed onward. report.html is fully self-contained: thumbnails inlined, no external requests, mailable as a single file. Thumbnails live in a .thumbs.json sidecar so the JSON stays small.

Technical differences are kept apart

SDR→HDR, a codec change, a channel-layout change: these are real and worth reporting, but they are not edits. Mixing them into the timeline is how a tool loses a user's trust — a format re-wrap that reports as forty-two replaced scenes is worse than no tool at all.

Stage 6 · optional, and last

The model writes the sentence. It never finds the change.

The detection is finished — timecodes, types and boundaries all decided — before a local model is asked anything. Its only job is turning delete · 00:12:04 → 00:12:41 · 4 shots into "a four-shot exchange in the corridor is missing from the TV cut."

Stage 6 is off by default, and the build order required stages 0–5 and 7 to work with it disabled. Turn the model off and you lose prose. You lose no finding, no timecode and no region.

Why it is bounded this way

  • Reproducible. The same two files give the same regions, run to run, machine to machine.
  • Auditable. A disputed result traces to a Hamming distance and a scoring rule, not to a sampled token.
  • Honest about failure. A hallucinated description is obviously wrong beside its own thumbnails; a hallucinated timecode would be undetectable.
THE CONTRACT WITH THE MODEL

✓ What it is asked

"Here are thumbnails from one region of one version, and its type and duration. Describe what is shown, in one sentence."

✗ What it is never asked

"Here are two sets of frames — tell us what changed." Comparison is assembled in Python from values the pipeline already computed. The model is never the judge of difference.

Hearing the difference, not just reading about it

An audio_changed region is a claim about sound, and the only real way to check it is to listen. Both versions get a play button in their own column, side by side, and only one plays at a time. Clips are cut on demand — most regions are never played, and cutting every one up front would slow every comparison for a feature used on a handful. They are cut from the original where it is still on disk: one lossy generation instead of two, and every audio track rather than whichever one ffmpeg would have picked.

What broke · and what the fix measured

Three bugs that only real delivery formats could find.

01 — BROADCAST MXF

Five of six audio tracks silently vanished

Broadcast MXF carries discrete mono tracks — dialogue, music, effects, each its own stream. ffmpeg -i src maps only the first. The rest disappeared from playback and from the fingerprint, so a change confined to track 2 was simply not there.

Fix: amerge across every track before hashing.

0 → 26/64 a music-only change, before and after
02 — HDR MASTERS

Every shot faked a replace

PQ-encoded pixels were going into an 8-bit BT.709 proxy untouched. An HDR/SDR pair of the same edit measured 12/64 — inside the weak band, out of the match band — so the alignment refused to call anything equal.

Fix: tone-map in the proxy — zscale → tonemap=hable → bt709.

12 → 6/64 same edit, HDR vs SDR — now zero regions
03 — CACHED PROXIES

A fixed pipeline, still broken output

Both fixes above change how a proxy is built. A cached proxy from before the fix is still a valid file, so it was reused — and the bug survived its own fix, invisibly.

Fix: PROXY_PIPELINE_VERSION is part of the cache key. Bump it and every stale proxy is rebuilt.

The general lesson: a cache keyed on inputs alone is wrong the moment the transform can change.
One more, worth stating because it cuts the other way: the leading theory for the audio problem was double-lossy encoding. Measured, it cost 0.4 dB — nothing. It was not the cause, and saying so was more useful than claiming the credit.

Stated plainly

What this proof of concept does not do.

KNOWN BLIND SPOT

Colour-only changes are invisible

phash is greyscale by construction. A re-grade, a colourisation, a saturation pass — all measure 0/64. The tool will report "no change" and be confidently wrong. This is the single most important limitation to know before trusting a result.

SCOPE

Two versions, one pair at a time

No fingerprint database, no dedup index across a library, no three-way compare. Deliberately out of scope for a POC — and the alignment generalises to more than two sequences only at real cost.

VALIDATION GAP

Synthetic fixtures, not real film

Every fixture is generated. The MXF and HDR cases are synthetic too. Shot detection on real footage — dissolves, handheld, fast cutting — has not been measured, and neither has the videotoolbox encode path on actual Apple silicon.

SENSITIVITY

One threshold, one alphabet

Shot detection runs at a fixed threshold of 27.0. One frame represents an entire shot, so a change that happens within a long take, away from its midpoint, can be missed entirely.

None of these is a surprise or a defect — they are the consequences of choices made to get a real end-to-end path working. They are written down because a tool that hides its blind spots is more dangerous than one that has none.

Where this goes next

Future expansion.

NEAR — CLOSES A KNOWN GAP

A colour signal beside the luma one

A per-shot colour histogram, compared on its own axis, turns the greyscale blind spot into a reported graded region. Same architecture — a second annotation pass, not a change to the alignment.

NEAR — TRUSTWORTHINESS

Validation on real footage

Run the suite against genuine multi-version titles. Measure shot detection through dissolves and fades, and settle the within-a-long-take case that check 7 currently only reports.

NEAR — FITS THE WORKFLOW

EDL / AAF export

Regions already carry timecodes on both sides. Emitting an EDL puts the findings straight into the tool the editor already has open, instead of a report they must retype from.

MEDIUM — SENSITIVITY

Multi-frame shots, sub-shot alignment

Hash several frames per shot rather than the midpoint alone, and align within a matched pair to locate a trim inside a long take. Costs linearly; buys back the sensitivity given up at stage 4.

MEDIUM — SCALE

A fingerprint store

Fingerprints are already cached per file. Persist them and the question changes from "how do these two differ" to "which version in the library is this, and what is it closest to" — with no re-decode.

MEDIUM — COVERAGE

Subtitles and burn-ins as a third track

Compliance and localisation differences often live entirely in the text layer. It aligns exactly like the other two — same sequence, same algorithm, third annotation.

LONGER — N-WAY

Beyond two versions

A version tree across every cut of a title: which derives from which, what each one uniquely contains. Progressive alignment against a reference, the way multiple sequence alignment actually does it.

LONGER — INTEGRATION

Headless mode in a delivery pipeline

The JSON contract is already stable and the stages are already a graph. Running on ingest — every new master diffed against its predecessor automatically — is a scheduling problem, not a research one.

LONGER — THE MODEL

Better prose, same boundary

Larger local models, shot-level captions, scene-level summaries. The boundary does not move: the model describes what alignment found. Detection stays arithmetic.

In one slide

What was built, and why it holds up.

  • Two cuts of a title in, a timecoded plain-English report out.
  • The hard part is sequence alignment, and it is solved with a real algorithm — Needleman-Wunsch, optimal, vectorised, no model anywhere near it.
  • Picture decides the timeline; audio and technical metadata annotate it without ever corrupting it.
  • The LLM is optional and last. Switch it off and you lose sentences, not findings.
  • Every claim on these slides is a measured number from this codebase — including the ones that are unflattering.
  • It runs entirely on one laptop. Nothing leaves the machine.
THE SHAPE OF THE ARGUMENT

A version-diff tool is only worth having if its output can be trusted without re-checking it by hand. That is why the detection path is deterministic arithmetic, why the blind spots are written down rather than buried, and why the model was kept out of the part that decides what changed.

Everything in the expansion list adds signals and reach. None of it requires moving that boundary.
